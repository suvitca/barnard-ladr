Before the 1970s, deaf people in Nicaragua communicated with their families using *home sign*: rudimentary signs developed within a household for basic communication. Outside the home, deaf people struggled to communicate with each other.

Now, just a few decades later, Nicaragua has a rich, developed sign language. Nicaraguan Sign Language was not imported from another country. It was not invented by teachers, parents, or deaf adults and passed to students through deliberate instruction. The language arose naturally, among a generation of young Nicaraguans who needed to communicate with each other.

## The first generation

Changes in special education in the late 1970s and early 1980s transformed the social lives of deaf people. A new, larger school for special education opened in the San Judas neighborhood of Managua, bringing together more deaf children than ever before, and at younger ages. The initial group was 50 children, aged 4 to 14. Instead of spending most of their time at home using home sign, these children were now engaging with each other, and they needed a way to communicate.

This first generation was a generation of pioneers. At first, communication was as difficult for them as it had been for earlier generations. Our research has found that as they interacted, they began to change the gestures and home signs they were using. Their vocabulary grew quickly, just as when a young child learns to talk. Their signs became more systematic, more regular, and less gestural, and their sentences became much more complex.

<blockquote class="pull">Nicaraguan Sign Language came from the same place that all languages came from: human minds, trying to connect with other human minds.</blockquote>

## A language passed down

By the end of the 1980s, when this generation reached adulthood, their signing was rapid and fluent. The language had grown to resemble other languages around the world, and could express ideas as complex as any other language.

What happened in the next decade was just as important. The first generation, now several hundred adults, passed their language to a new generation of children. Children entering the school in the 1990s found a linguistic world rich with signing that had been developing for over ten years. Each new class watched, learned from the children before them, and became fluent within a few years. New words and phrases — even new ways of putting signs together — entered the language as it was used on buses, in the schoolyard, at the deaf association, and in deaf people's homes.

The community has grown with the language. Today more than 1,500 deaf children and adults use Nicaraguan Sign Language.
